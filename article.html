Bahay na bato

Image
Bahay na bato
Also known as Balay na bato
Period Late 16th – early 20th century
Materials Stone, coral, hardwood, capiz shell
Region Philippines

Bahay na bato (literally “house of stone”) is a traditional Filipino house style that developed during the Spanish colonial period. Typically two stories tall, it pairs a stone or brick ground floor with a wooden upper floor, a form that blends indigenous Austronesian house-building with Spanish and Chinese construction techniques.[1]

Origins

The form grew out of the bahay kubo, the indigenous stilt house built from bamboo and thatch. Spanish colonial rule introduced stone masonry and tile roofing, while Chinese merchants in port towns contributed brickmaking and joinery. The result was a hybrid, not a Spanish house transplanted, but a new form shaped by local climate, materials, and earthquake risk.[2]

Early examples appear in the late 16th century, but the style reached its peak in the 19th century, when the galleon trade and agricultural exports created a wealthy merchant class that could afford to build them.

Architecture

The ground floor, called the zaguan, is built from volcanic tuff, brick, or coral stone. It was used for storage, carriages, and sometimes livestock, practical uses kept out of the living quarters above.

The upper floor is timber, with wide plank flooring and ventanillas, small sliding panels set below the main windows that let air circulate while keeping rain out. Window openings are filled with translucent capiz shell instead of glass, which softens the tropical light.

Many houses include an azotea, an open terrace extending from the rear, often with a cistern beneath it.

Regional variations

The best-preserved concentration is in Vigan, Ilocos Sur, where Chinese-Filipino merchant houses line the Calle Crisologo. Distinct traditions also survive in Taal, Batangas; Carcar, Cebu; and Iloilo City.[3]

Decline and revival

The American colonial period brought concrete, galvanized iron, and a preference for newer materials. Many houses were destroyed during the Second World War. In recent decades, heritage campaigns have restored surviving examples, and Vigan was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1999.
